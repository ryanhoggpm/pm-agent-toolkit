# USA employment law reference

Legal guardrails embedded in every hiring-manager output. Read and apply before generating any candidate evaluation, interview question, or research report. This is working guidance for hiring hygiene, not legal advice; route real legal questions to counsel.

## Federal protections (always apply, regardless of state)

### Protected classes under federal law

**EEOC-enforced. Never ask about, flag, or factor into decisions:**

- Race, color, national origin (Title VII)
- Sex, gender, pregnancy, childbirth, or related conditions (Title VII / PDA)
- Religion or religious practices (Title VII)
- Age 40 and older (ADEA)
- Disability or medical history (ADA); describe job requirements and ask whether the candidate can perform them with or without accommodation, never about the disability itself
- Genetic information, family medical history (GINA)
- Veteran status (VEVRAA)
- Citizenship status (INA); you CAN ask "are you legally authorized to work in the US," you may NOT ask where someone is from or their visa type

**If research surfaces protected-class information** (photos, religious affiliations, pregnancy announcements, disability disclosures), do not include it in any output. Note that protected-class information was encountered and set aside.

### FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act)

Applies when a third-party Consumer Reporting Agency runs the background check:

- Written disclosure and authorization before ordering the report
- Adverse action process: pre-adverse notice + copy of report + Summary of Rights, a reasonable wait (typically 5 business days), then the adverse action notice
- Candidates may dispute inaccurate information

Direct web research by this skill is not a CRA product, so FCRA doesn't technically apply, but EEOC standards still do. Treat web research as supplementary job-relevant context, never as a formal background check.

### Title VII process rules

- Requirements must be job-related and consistent with business necessity
- Screening criteria with disparate impact on protected classes are prohibited unless job-related
- Ask the same questions of every candidate for the same role

## State layer: check your jurisdiction

States add protections and procedures on top of federal law. Before the first output for a new role, confirm the work location's rules on: additional protected classes, salary history bans, pay transparency requirements, ban-the-box timing, and criminal-history assessment standards. Record what applies in the role's folder so every later output uses it.

### Worked example: Minnesota

- **MHRA adds:** marital status, public-assistance status, sexual orientation, local human-rights-commission activity, and age at all ages (not just 40+)
- **Pay transparency:** employers with 30+ employees must post salary ranges (since 2024-01-01); salary history may not be asked or used
- **Criminal history:** only job-related convictions count, never a blanket disqualifier, and an individualized assessment is required (nature of crime, time elapsed, rehabilitation, relevance to duties)
- **Ban the box** binds public employers; for private employers, best practice is still no application-stage criminal questions, address post-offer

## Can and cannot ask: quick reference

| Topic | Can ask | Cannot ask |
|---|---|---|
| Work authorization | "Are you legally authorized to work in the US?" | "Are you a citizen?" "Where are you from?" "What visa?" |
| Availability | "Can you work the required hours?" "Available to start on [date]?" | "Do you have children?" "Childcare arrangements?" |
| Criminal history | Post-offer, job-relevant convictions with individualized assessment | Application-stage questions (best practice) |
| Physical ability | "Can you perform the essential functions with or without accommodation?" | "Do you have a disability?" "Ever filed workers' comp?" |
| Education | Degree verification, field of study | Graduation year (age proxy) |
| References | Employment history, performance, skills | Medical, family, religion, personal beliefs |
| Social media | Job-relevant professional conduct; public statements contradicting the resume | Protected-class information encountered incidentally |

## Social media / public records research: permitted scope

**Permitted:**

- Public LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio, professional sites: work quality and professional conduct
- Public statements directly contradicting resume claims (education, titles, dates, accomplishments)
- Professional misconduct directly relevant to the role
- Confirmed, job-relevant criminal convictions, subject to the state's assessment requirements

**Not permitted:**

- Anything revealing protected-class information
- Private accounts or anything behind a login
- Information older than 7 years (FCRA guidance, applied as best practice)
- Speculation about health, family, religion, politics, personal beliefs

**Required reporting format:** state the search performed; report job-relevant findings only; flag protected-class encounters as set aside; rate each finding confirmed/unconfirmed; tie each finding to a specific job requirement.

## Interview question legality

**Illegal or high-risk:** age or graduation year; marriage, kids, family plans; "where are you from originally"; health conditions or work injuries; religion or religious holidays; arrests without conviction; citizenship.

**Legal alternatives:** age → required experience level. Family plans → "the role requires occasional travel and evening work; is that workable?" Disability → "can you perform these specific functions with or without accommodation?" Religion → "the schedule requires [days/hours]; can you commit to it?"

## Adverse action checklist

Before declining a candidate, confirm:

1. Decision rests on job-related criteria documented in the JD
2. Same criteria applied consistently to all candidates
3. No protected-class information factored in
4. If criminal history was a factor: the state's individualized assessment was completed
5. If a CRA report was used: FCRA adverse action process followed
6. Decision and rationale documented (best practice: 3 years record retention)
